[Three.js](https://threejs.org/) draws 3D scenes with WebGL, and [react-three-fiber](https://r3f.docs.pmnd.rs/)
writes them as React components. `@sportsdataverse/sdvplot/three` builds what a sports scene needs as plain mesh
buffers: a sporty playing surface as one flat mesh, and a tube along any 3D path in a team's colour, with a helper
that turns a Statcast pitch into its path. It never imports three.js (install `three` 0.186.1 or later beside it, an
optional peer), so the buffers can be built anywhere, a server included: `Float32Array` positions and colours and a
`Uint32Array` of indices, ready for a three.js `BufferGeometry`. `surfaceMesh` draws through
`@sportsdataverse/sporty`, which this subpath imports when it loads; the team colours are synchronous, so load the
league first (`loadLeague`).

For react-three-fiber, `@sportsdataverse/sdvplot/react` draws the buffers with two components, `<Surface3D>` and
`<Trajectory>`, to put inside your own `<Canvas>`. They load `three` and `@react-three/fiber` (9.8.1 or later, which
needs React 19) only when they first render, so `sdvplot/react` imports and server-renders without them; a missing
one throws `OptionalDependencyError` then, naming what to install. That failed load is kept until the page reloads, so
reload after installing the peer. A server render draws nothing: the scene draws on the client. A `<Trajectory>` in
a team's colour needs the team's league loaded (`loadLeague`): until it is, it draws nothing, with one warning, and in
the browser it loads the league and then draws.

## Units and the frame

Everything is in world units, never px: the surface's own (feet for a baseball field or a rink, metres for a pitch),
and the tube's `radius` in the same units as its points. A tube is as thick as its radius whatever the camera, and
`trajectoryTube` refuses a `width`, `size` or `lineWidth` with an `InputError` that says so. The ground is the x-y
plane at z = 0 with z up, in sporty's frame: a baseball field has home plate's point at the origin and +y towards
centre field, the other surfaces their centre at the origin. three.js's own default is y up, so give the camera
`up={[0, 0, 1]}` (or turn the group -90 degrees about x).

## A field, flat, in one mesh

`surfaceMesh(sport, league, o)` is sporty's surface as one triangle mesh: positions, vertex colours (r, g, b, a in
linear sRGB, three.js's working colour space, so the drawn colour is sporty's) and indices, with `loops` naming where
each feature's triangles sit. The surface's background colour, if it has one (a baseball field's outfield grass), is
the first loop, over the whole extent; then each closed loop of each feature in sporty's drawing order. sporty draws
some features as two or more loops in one path (a faceoff circle's half rings, the two strips of the NHL goaltender's
trapezoid), and a ring that crosses itself (from unusual `updates`) is split at the crossing; each loop is filled on
its own, so nothing is filled on the wrong side. Hidden features and text are left out, and sporty's heights are flattened: z is 0 throughout. `o` goes to
sporty's `surface()` (`colorUpdates`, `units`, `rotation`, ...).

Every loop lies in the same plane, so draw the mesh in order, without depth writes and before the rest of the scene:
later loops then paint over earlier ones with no z-fighting. `<Surface3D sport league options>` does exactly that: a
`MeshBasicMaterial` with `vertexColors`, `transparent`, `depthWrite: false`, `toneMapped: false` and both sides,
`renderOrder` -1.

## Pitches in flight

`statcastPitchPath(pitch)` turns a Baseball Savant (Statcast) pitch into its flight, `[x, y, z]` points in feet from
the release to home plate. Statcast describes each pitch with the nine-parameter fit of the PITCHf/x era: a constant
acceleration (`ax`, `ay`, `az`) and the velocity (`vx0`, `vy0`, `vz0`) where the pitch crosses y = 50 ft, so its
position is p(t) = p₅₀ + v₀t + ½at² ([Savant's CSV documentation](https://baseballsavant.mlb.com/csv-docs); A. M.
Nathan, [Determining Pitch Movement from PITCHf/x Data](https://baseball.physics.illinois.edu/Movement.pdf), 2012).
Savant's CSV leaves out where the pitch was at y = 50, so it is solved from where the fit crosses the plate
(`plate_x`, `plate_z`), and the path runs from `release_pos_y` to that plane in equal steps of time (`steps`, default
40). Savant measures that crossing at the plate's front edge through 2025 and at its middle from 2026, so the path
reads the pitch's `game_year` (or takes `plate: "front" | "middle"`; that rule is from Savant's documentation of its MLB search, so pass `plate`
for its World Baseball Classic or minor-league searches). Every field must be a number: drop rows with an empty CSV
cell first, since `Number("")` is 0. Traced back to the release, it lands on Savant's
own release point within its 0.01 ft rounding; the tests check that on real pitches of both seasons. Statcast's
frame is sporty's baseball frame, x to the catcher's right (the first-base side), so the path draws over the field
unconverted.

`trajectoryTube(points, { radius, team, league })` is a tube along the path: a ring of `radialSegments` vertices
(default 12) round each point, carried along without twisting, its colour the team's. `<Trajectory points radius team
league>` draws it unlit and not tone-mapped, so the team's colour shows exactly as it is. Pass `color` instead of a
team for a colour of your own.

## Team colours against the scene

`teamColorPalette(league, teams, { background })` gives one colour per team, as in the other adapters. With the
colour the marks are seen against as `background` (the example uses the field's grass), every colour keeps at least
3:1 contrast against it, as in dark mode, and the teams aim to stay apart (a CIE76 ΔE of 20 or more), through
sdvplot's shared team palette; if no candidate is both, it keeps the readable colour and warns once. A team listed
twice gets one colour; a team that does not resolve takes `fallback` (default `#808080`) as given, its contrast
unchecked, and stays out of the palette. `trajectoryTube` takes the same `background` for a single team's tube.

## Without React

The buffers are what a three.js `BufferGeometry` takes. For a surface, set `position` from `positions` (3 per
vertex), `color` from `colors` (4 per vertex) and the index from `indices`, and draw it with the material above; for a
tube, `position`, `normal` (from `normals`) and the index, with a `MeshBasicMaterial` in the tube's `color` and
`toneMapped: false`.

## Reading back what was drawn

There is no `drawnMarks` here: the buffers are the record. `surfaceMesh(...).loops` names each loop with its sporty
colour and where its vertices and triangles are; a tube carries its `color`, `radius` and path `length`, so a test can
check them without a WebGL context.
